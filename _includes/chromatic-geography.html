How is this done?

Each photo is analysed for its colour distribution and placed at a point in a higher-dimensional colour space. In this space, photos with similar colour palettes are grouped closer together. Every pair of photos is then compared and tested for how much colour needs to be moved, and how far, to turn one palette into the other. This gives rise to a notion of distance between photos based on colour, and one can project these distances down onto two-dimensional space. Note that the closest \(n\) neighbours aren't necessarily the closest in the 2D plane, but rather, the closest in the higher-dimensional colour space (the 2d projection doesn't preserve those distances perfectly!)

Mathematical explanation:

Each image is represented by a probability measure \(\mu_i\) over CIELAB colour space (separate into \(K\) colour buckets and normalise). Pairwise distances between photos are then measured using \(1\)-Wasserstein distance with transport cost given by the usual Euclidean metric on CIELAB, yielding a distance matrix \(D_{ij}=W_1(\mu_i,\mu_j)\). Classical multidimensional scaling can then be applied to \(D\); writing \(J=I-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\), one obtains a doubly-centered matrix \(B=-\frac{1}{2}J(D\circ D)J\). From here, perform an eigendecomposition to get \(B=V\Lambda V^\top\) and take only the leading two positive eigendirections \(X_{2D}=V_2\Lambda_2^{1/2}\). The resulting 2D projection approximates pairwise chromatic distances between photos.
